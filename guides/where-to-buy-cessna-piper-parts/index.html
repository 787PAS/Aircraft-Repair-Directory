Where to Buy Cessna and Piper Parts

OEM dealers, salvage yards, reproduction suppliers, and eBay — what each is good for

The sourcing landscape for legacy Cessna and Piper aircraft

The Cessna 150, 172, 182, and Piper PA-22 were all out of production decades ago, but the parts supply for these aircraft is unusually robust by vintage standards. The reason is simple: the combined installed base runs to tens of thousands of aircraft, many of which are still flying and require ongoing maintenance. That demand has sustained an aftermarket ecosystem that covers everything from rivets to major structural assemblies.

The right sourcing channel depends on the part category. Here's a breakdown of the main options and what each one is — and isn't — good for.

The main sourcing channels

OEM / Dealer

Cessna / Textron parts dealers

New parts with full traceability. Most expensive. Best for life-limited parts, fluid-system consumables, and anything where provenance matters most.

Serviceable

Aviation salvage yards

Parts removed from parted-out aircraft with 8130-3 paperwork. Best for structural parts, trim panels, and hardware. Large price advantage over new.

PMA New

PMA parts suppliers

New third-party parts under FAA approval. Legal equivalents to OEM for approved applications. Good for brake hardware, filters, exhaust, and many fasteners.

Verify

eBay and private sales

Broad selection at low prices. Requires careful verification of part numbers, applicability, and paperwork. Good for hardware; use caution on structural or safety-critical parts.

OEM dealers: when the cost is worth it

Textron Aviation (which owns the Cessna type certificates) and its dealer network are the primary source for new, OEM Cessna parts. Piper Aircraft similarly supports its legacy fleet through a dealer and distributor network.

OEM parts come with a new-parts certificate and carry the full manufacturer traceability. For anything where failure would be life-critical — fuel selector valves, brake master cylinders, critical flight control hardware, and seat-rail assemblies subject to AD — the documentation alone often justifies the premium. For commodity items (hardware, gaskets, O-rings), the cost difference over aftermarket is rarely worth it.

Aviation salvage yards: the workhouse of the aftermarket

Licensed aircraft salvage yards are the largest source of affordable airframe parts for legacy Cessna and Piper aircraft. When an aircraft is damaged beyond economical repair or reaches the end of its registered life, the airframe is parted out — systems, avionics, structural panels, control hardware, and furnishings are removed, inspected, tagged with FAA Form 8130-3, and sold as serviceable parts.

What salvage yards do well

  • Structural sheet-metal panels, skins, and ribs in serviceable condition
  • Control hardware — pulleys, bellcranks, cable guides, trim mechanisms
  • Interior components — seats, upholstery panels, overhead liners
  • Avionics and instruments (though calibration needs to be verified)
  • Cowl panels, fairings, and fiberglass components

What salvage yards are not the right source for

  • Rubber seals, O-rings, hoses, and gaskets — buy these new regardless of price
  • Life-limited parts without complete time/cycle history documentation
  • Parts subject to ADs that require modification to a revised part number
Always specify your aircraft serial number when ordering from a salvage yard. Cessna and Piper changed part numbers between production years; a part from a 1965 150 may look identical to the 1973 part but carry a different effectivity code. A good yard will check applicability for you — if they don't ask for your serial number, ask yourself whether they're checking.

PMA parts: new hardware at aftermarket prices

Parts Manufacturer Approval (PMA) parts are new, FAA-approved alternatives to OEM parts. They're manufactured under an FAA production approval and are legal replacements for the specific aircraft/model applications listed in the PMA holder's approval data.

PMA is especially well-developed for:

  • Brake system parts — Cleveland and Matco alternatives cover most Cessna and Piper brake assemblies at significant savings
  • Exhaust components — several PMA exhaust manufacturers cover the common Continental O-200/O-300 and Lycoming O-320/O-360 installations
  • Fuel system consumables — fuel cap seals, strainer bowl gaskets, and selector valve O-rings
  • Filters and spark plugs — among the most widely PMA-approved categories

eBay and private aviation marketplaces

eBay has become a significant marketplace for general aviation parts, particularly hardware, small accessories, avionics, and interior components. The selection is broad and prices are often well below dealer levels — but the verification burden falls entirely on the buyer.

Practical rules for eBay aircraft parts:

  • Always ask for the complete part number as stamped or labeled on the part — not just what the listing title says
  • For anything structural or safety-critical, ask for 8130-3 paperwork before bidding; walk away if the seller can't provide it
  • Check the part number against your aircraft's IPC effectivity codes — see our guide to reading an IPC for how to do this
  • Avoid anything described as "for display" or "as-is" for installation on a certified aircraft
  • Standard hardware (AN/NAS/MS parts) doesn't require 8130-3; inspect it yourself

Type clubs and owner communities

For hard-to-find or obsolete parts, the Cessna and Piper owner communities are often the fastest path. The Cessna Pilots Association (CPA), various Piper owner groups, and model-specific forums all maintain active classified sections where members sell parts from aircraft they're rebuilding or dismantling.

Community sources carry the same verification requirements as eBay or any private sale — paperwork, part number confirmation, and applicability check — but the sellers tend to be knowledgeable owners who can provide more context about where the part came from and how it was used.

Start with the part number. Every sourcing conversation should begin with the exact part number from your aircraft's IPC — not a description, not a photograph. Use this site to look up the correct part number by aircraft and figure, then take that number to your supplier of choice.
