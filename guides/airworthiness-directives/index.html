Understanding Airworthiness Directives for Light Aircraft

What ADs are, how to find them, and what they mean for parts and maintenance

All Parts › Guides › Airworthiness Directives

What an Airworthiness Directive is

An Airworthiness Directive (AD) is a legally enforceable regulation issued by the FAA (or a foreign authority for foreign-type-certificated aircraft) that requires specific action on a certificated aircraft, engine, propeller, or appliance. ADs are issued when the FAA determines that an unsafe condition exists and is likely to exist in other products of the same type.

Unlike a service bulletin — which is a manufacturer's recommendation — an AD is mandatory. Operating an aircraft with an outstanding, uncomplied-with AD is illegal and invalidates the aircraft's airworthiness certificate. ADs must be tracked, complied with, and recorded in the aircraft logbooks.

The two main types

One-time compliance

Requires a single action — inspect and replace a part, modify a system, or establish a new operating limitation. Once complied with and logged, it's done unless the AD specifies re-inspection intervals.

Recurring compliance

Requires repetitive action at specified intervals — inspect every 100 hours, replace every 500 hours, or at every annual inspection. Must be tracked and performed on schedule for the aircraft to remain airworthy.

How ADs affect parts sourcing

ADs matter for parts in two specific ways: they can require replacement of a part with a specific new or modified version, and they can make a part you're considering buying ineligible for installation if it doesn't incorporate the required modification.

Parts superseded by AD

Some ADs require the removal and replacement of a specific part number with a revised part number that incorporates a design change. If you buy a used part off the shelf and that part number was superseded by an AD, you may be holding a part that cannot legally be reinstalled — even if it's physically identical to what it replaces. This is most common with fuel system components, flight control hardware, and certain structural fittings.

Before buying a structural or safety-critical used part: look up the part number in the FAA's AD database to confirm it hasn't been superseded or restricted by an AD. A reputable parts dealer will flag this, but not all will.

Life-limited parts

Some ADs establish mandatory retirement limits for specific parts — a maximum number of flight hours, cycles, or calendar years after which the part must be removed from service regardless of apparent condition. When buying a used part subject to a life limit, you need documented time-since-new (TSN) or time-since-overhaul (TSO) to know how much life remains. A part with no traceable history is essentially zero-remaining-life for the purposes of legal installation.

Common AD categories for Cessna and Piper singles

Certain systems generate a disproportionate share of the AD activity for the Cessna 150, 172, 182, and Piper PA-22 and PA-28 families:

  • Fuel systems — selector valve O-rings, tank sump drains, fuel cap venting, and crossfeed plumbing have been the subject of multiple ADs across Cessna and Piper models.
  • Flight controls — aileron and elevator attachment fittings, bellcrank and pulley brackets, and trim tab hinges on aging aluminum airframes.
  • Landing gear — nose gear shimmy dampers, main gear attach fittings, and wheel assembly corrosion are recurring themes.
  • Engine installation — exhaust system cracks, engine mount corrosion, magneto impulse coupling inspection, and carburetor float issues are common AD drivers for Continental O-200 and O-300 powered aircraft.
  • Seat tracks — the infamous seat-rail engagement AD affecting multiple Cessna models requires compliance at every annual and at specific intervals.

How to find ADs that apply to your aircraft

The FAA maintains a free searchable database of all current ADs:

  1. Go to the FAA's Regulatory and Guidance Library (rgl.faa.gov) or search "FAA AD database."
  2. Search by aircraft make, model, or type certificate number to get the list of applicable ADs.
  3. Cross-check the AD applicability statement against your aircraft's serial number — not all ADs apply to every serial number in a model range.
  4. For engine and propeller ADs, search separately using the engine make and model.

Many IA (Inspection Authorization) mechanics use commercial AD tracking services (such as Av-Data or similar) that maintain a current, serial-number-specific AD compliance list. If you're purchasing an aircraft, request the AD compliance record from the seller and verify it against the current database.

AD compliance and the annual inspection

During every annual inspection, the IA is required to verify AD compliance for the airframe, engine, propeller, and all installed appliances. The IA will check the aircraft logbooks for compliance entries and confirm that recurring ADs are current. Any open ADs must be complied with before the IA can sign off the annual.

If you're performing owner-performed preventive maintenance between annuals, you are responsible for not introducing a condition that violates an AD — for example, by reinstalling a part that was superseded.

Important: This guide is an introduction for general awareness, not legal or maintenance advice. AD compliance must be performed by or under the supervision of an FAA-certificated mechanic or IA. When in doubt, ask your A&P.
Tip: When a part page on this site shows "New only" or "New recommended" in the buying advice section, it's often because that part category is subject to recurring ADs, life limits, or known failure modes that make used sourcing risky. Check the FAA AD database for the specific part number before making a final decision.
