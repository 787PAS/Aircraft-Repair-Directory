Cessna 150 Parts Reference Guide

Part number families, year breaks, and sourcing advice for owners and mechanics

All Parts › Guides › Cessna 150 Parts

Why the Cessna 150 is one of the best-supported legacy aircraft

More than 23,000 Cessna 150s were built between 1958 and 1977, making it one of the most common light aircraft in the world. That production volume created a large installed base — and a correspondingly deep aftermarket. Parts that are no longer manufactured new are often available as serviceable removals from the thousands of aircraft that have been parted out over the decades, and many structural and sheet-metal parts are still being reproduced by the light aircraft aftermarket.

That said, the Cessna 150 went through meaningful engineering changes across its production run. Knowing which year-range your airframe falls into is the first step to finding a part that will actually fit and be legal to install.

Always verify by serial number. Cessna's IPC effectivity codes (the "usable-on" column) tell you which specific serial-number ranges a part applies to. A 1969-catalog part number is not automatically correct for a 1971 airframe even if the aircraft look identical from the outside.

The Continental O-200 engine

Most Cessna 150s are powered by the Continental O-200-A (100 hp). This engine has its own separate parts catalog, published by Continental Motors, and engine parts are sourced through engine overhaul shops and Continental dealers rather than Cessna airframe parts suppliers.

The airframe IPC covers engine installation components — the engine mount, baffles, cowl, exhaust, and oil lines — but not internal engine parts. If you're looking for cylinders, pistons, or accessory drive parts, you need the Continental O-200 overhaul manual and parts catalog, not the Cessna IPC.

High-wear and commonly replaced parts

After decades in service, Cessna 150s tend to need the same categories of parts repeatedly. These are the areas worth knowing well:

Control system

The 150 uses conventional cable-and-pulley flight controls. Aileron, elevator, and rudder cables are the most commonly replaced items — inspect for broken strands, corrosion near the swage fittings, and wear at the pulley contact points. The elevator trim cable system deserves attention on older aircraft; the drum and sprocket mechanism can wear significantly.

Landing gear and brake system

The 150 has a tubular steel main gear and a steerable nosewheel. Common parts include the nosewheel shimmy damper, main gear fairings (wheel pants), brake discs and linings, master cylinders, and the Cleveland or McCauley wheel assemblies. Brake hoses should be treated as consumables and replaced new; the short hose from the caliper to the rigid brake line is a routine maintenance item.

Fuel system

The 150 uses two wing tanks feeding a simple fuel selector. The selector valve O-rings and the fuel strainer bowl gasket are inexpensive parts that should be replaced at every annual. Fuel tank bladders on earlier aircraft may require replacement if they show cracking or delamination.

Airframe skin and structure

The 150's aluminum airframe is robust, but corrosion in the lower fuselage belly skins, along the wing-root area, and in the horizontal stabilizer attachment area is common on aircraft that operated in humid or coastal environments. Doublers, replacement skin panels, and structural repair materials are available from light aircraft parts suppliers.

Part number format

Most Cessna-manufactured airframe parts use a numeric format: a base number followed by a dash and a suffix (e.g., 0511289-4). The dash number typically indicates a variant — left-hand vs. right-hand, a different material, or a revised design. Standard hardware items use the AN/NAS/MS prefix system common across all U.S. general aviation aircraft.

Some parts will cross-reference between Cessna 150 and 172 catalogs where the aircraft share common components — particularly control hardware, standard brackets, and avionics trays. It is worth checking the 172 catalog if a 150 part is hard to find, and vice versa.

Where to look for hard-to-find parts

  • Licensed salvage yards — The largest source of low-cost serviceable parts. Reputable yards provide 8130-3 paperwork on removed components.
  • CESSNA PILOTS ASSOCIATION (CPA) — The owner's association maintains classifieds and technical resources specifically for Cessna singles.
  • Type club members and forums — Other 150 owners are often the fastest way to locate obsolete parts; the community is large and active.
  • Light aircraft parts reproduction houses — Several companies produce structural parts and skins for the 150 series under FAA-PMA or field-approval basis.
  • eBay and aviation classifieds — Useful for hardware and small parts; verify serial-number applicability and ask for paperwork before purchasing anything structural.
Tip: When ordering from a salvage yard, give them your aircraft's serial number, not just the model. Cessna 150 production spanned nearly 20 years and the effectivity codes matter — a part pulled from a 1965 aircraft may not be correct for a 1974.
